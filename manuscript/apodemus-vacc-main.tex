	\section{abstract}

	\textbf{Vaccination is the most effective medical intervention to prevent infectious disease and protect public health. However, most new vaccines fail late in the clinical trial pipeline, and even established vaccines often perform poorly in populations that differ from those in which they were developed. This can lead to reduced vaccine responsiveness, breakthrough infections, and prevent or delay herd immunity. While the causes of vaccine hyporesponsiveness remain difficult to identify, quantify, and therefore address, numerous reports indicate a preponderant role of environmental factors, notably in rural vs urban populations. Here, we hypothesised that if the leading causes of vaccine hyporesponsiveness are environmental, they are likely to be exacerbated when individuals are under nutritional stress. To test how major environmental factors associated with wild (`rural') habitat affected vaccine responsiveness under differential dietary conditions, we used paired conspecific cohorts of laboratory-reared and wild wood mice (\textit{Apodemus sylvaticus}) vaccinated with a single or two doses of diphtheria toxoid vaccine formulated with alum, under supplemented vs. normal diets. We show that circulating vaccine-specific IgG1 antibodies were 47\% lower in a wild population than in individuals of the same species reared under controlled laboratory conditions. We also found that, regardless of habitat, substantial variation in vaccine responsiveness was explained by diet. However, contrary to our predictions, dietary supplementation resulted in lower vaccine responsiveness. Further, once habitat, diet, and sex were accounted for, increasing helminth infection burdens negatively affect vaccine-specific antibody production. While much variation within the habitat remains to be explained, our results indicate that the environment, chiefly dietary resources, plays a dramatic role in shaping the immune system's response to immunisation. Further, they suggest that laboratory settings may systematically overestimate vaccine performance by failing to capture natural environmental variability. Our study provides a robust, causally-explicit modelling approach and biological system to disentangle and quantify the complex factors that drive vaccine responsiveness in focal populations. It may also call for a need to systematically raise efficacy thresholds for initiating clinical phases of vaccine development. Test}

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Unanticipated variability in individual innate and adaptive immune responses to vaccination can lead to a lack of protection from the targeted disease, continued pathogen transmission to the wider population, and an increased risk of the evolution of vaccine escape~\cite{Greaney2021e,Harvey2021,Wang2021b,Kennedy2018, Vazquez-Prokopec2016}. Both intrinsic and extrinsic factors may impact vaccine responsiveness, including genetics~\cite{Posteraro2014} and epigenetics~\cite{Aracena2024}, sex~\cite{Flanagan2017}, reproductive status~\cite{Shah2019}, prior infections~\cite{Elias2008,Wait2020,Bowyer2020,Natukunda2022,Aquino2023}, and diet~\cite{Huda2019,Saleem2015,Yap2014,Temba2021}. Indeed, even temporary exposure to a natural environment drives maturation of B cells and enhances production of antibodies~\cite{Chen2025}. Such heterogeneities have stark implications for human health, manifesting for example in the substantial drop in vaccine immunogenicity and efficacy in low-income or rural populations relative to high-income or urban populations~\cite{Fourati2022,vanDorst2024}. Furthermore, helminth infections, for which the most at-risk individuals are most often found in rural and low-income populations~\cite{Hotez2008a,Asa2022}, can cause poor responsiveness to vaccines due to their ability to systemically suppress their host's immune system~\cite{Wammes2010, Maizels2016} although reports do not consistently detect a detrimental impact of helminth infection on vaccine efficacy~\cite{Wait2020}.

Thus, only a small minority of vaccines successfully transition from the preclinical stages of development through phase III trials and reach the mass market~\cite{Pronker2013}, and there are still no antihelminthic vaccines approved for humans. Furthermore, even the most successful vaccines can suffer substantial loss of efficacy, notably for people of different ancestry~\cite{Schaid2017} or different habitats~\cite{vanDorst2024} than the populations in which they are initially deployed. For example, individuals of Tanzanian ancestry were reported to have higher inflammatory and type 1 immune markers~\cite{Boahen2022} and responses to measles vaccination~\cite{Voigt2016} than those of European ancestry, while in rural populations the expression of BCG- and tetanus-specific cytokine recall and antibody responses was found to be markedly lower than in urban populations, while humoral responses to tetanus vaccination were higher~\cite{Zimmermann2019,Kabagenyi2020}. Similar patterns have been observed across multiple vaccines~\cite{vanDorst2024}. Taken together, these reports indicate that vaccine efficacy depends on both the vaccine and the recipients' characteristics, and that there are substantial gaps in our ability to predict and address vaccines  underperforming in different individuals and populations. Indeed, given the complex processes that regulate protective immune responses, it is unclear how all these factors interact with each other and the environment, and which among them could be manipulated to maximise or improve vaccination efficacy.

To increase the generalisability of experimental models used for immunology and vaccinology, recent efforts have been made to integrate natural variability into immunological research~\cite{Babayan2011,Beura2016,Graham2021,Rosshart2017,Rosshart2019}. These include exposing mice to richer, ``dirtier'' environments, littermates, and microbiota, which stimulates mice to develop more mature, and thus more representative, immune systems and responses to infection. However, there is still uncertainty about how these different sources of heterogeneity might interact to affect the immune system~\cite{Mangino2017}, and what consequences this might have for vaccine responsiveness. Thus, it is unclear how environmental factors and past stressors influence an organism's response to vaccination.

To disentangle the relative contributions of environmental variables to vaccine responsiveness, we combined experimental immunisations in laboratory and field settings with structural causal modelling. We conducted a randomised controlled vaccination and dietary supplementation experiment in a coupled lab-to-wild rodent study system, the wood mouse \textit{Apodemus sylvaticus}. Using the same assays and interventions in genetically outbred species across diverse habitats helped us isolate the environmental effects of living in the wild. This also controlled for the confounding effects of species, as well as reagent specificity, which can complicate comparisons. For this, we used a Diphtheria toxoid vaccine to ensure no prior exposure in the wild population could bias the vaccine-specific responses. Further, we have previously demonstrated in the same lab-to-wild model that high-quality diet supplementation drives a sustained reduction in helminth (\textit{Heligmosomoides polygyrus}) burdens and transmission, while also improving the efficacy of anthelmintic treatment~\cite{Sweeny2021}. We therefore sought to test the predictions that (i) vaccine responses in wild mice would be lower than in lab-reared wood mice, and (ii) that they'd be higher in diet-supplemented than normal-diet animals. Then, to quantify the causal effects of habitat, diet, sex, and parasite infection on vaccine-specific IgG1 responsiveness and to identify the paths through which their effects are mediated, we constructed a structural causal model (SCM)\cite{Pearl2009b}. This causal framework uses graphical models and structural equations to inform statistical adjustments~\cite{Glymour2019, Bareinboim2022} required for eliminating or reducing confounding, such as common cause (misattribution of causal effects), sampling bias (non-representative test population), and transportability bias (lack of generalisability). Under this causal framework, we used Bayesian models to estimate the average direct and indirect causes of vaccine efficacy loss when translating a vaccine from the laboratory to the wild. This approach helped track for how uncertainty and biological variability propagate through the causal structure.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Vaccination induced weaker antibody responses in the wild than in the laboratory}

Wild and laboratory \textit{A. sylvaticus} wood mice were given a high- or low-quality diet and then immunised with a diphtheria-alum vaccine (DTV) once (non-boosted) or twice (boosted). DTV-specific IgG1 serum concentrations were then measured by ELISA seven to 35 days post immunisation (see details in \nameref{sec:methods}; Figure~\ref{fig:design}).

DTV-specific IgG1 antibody responses were 46.9\% ± 0.1\% lower in the wild mice than their lab-based conspecifics, regardless of the vaccine regimen or diet (Figure~\ref{fig:igg1}A, \ref{fig:igg1}B `Hab'). While immunisation was most effective with two doses across both habitats, lab mice reached peak antibody levels with only a single dose (Figure~\ref{fig:igg1}A, `DA' vs `DD'; Lab boosted vs. Lab non-boosted OD = 0.2 ± 0.1 whereas Wild boosted vs. Wild non-boosted OD = 1.02 ± 0.23). Only mice receiving the diphtheria toxoid antigen generated DTV-specific IgG1, indicating no pre-existing immunity to this antigen in any of our study populations (Figure~\ref{fig:igg1}A, group `A').

However, this model does not explain how habitat and diet are driving poor vaccine responsiveness. Further, it does not account for potential confounding and mediating effects of mouse sex, reproductive status, body mass, body fat, or gastrointestinal parasite burdens, which we hypothesise also contribute to variation in vaccine responsiveness. To address these limitations, we constructed a structural causal model (SCM) of the processes generating variation in vaccine responsiveness $\mathbb{C}_{VE}$ in this system.

We tested the validity of the causal hypotheses represented in the DAG $\mathbb{G}_{VE}$ using the observed data (Fig~\ref{fig:DAGtikz}; and see methods section on \nameref{sec:construction}). For example, $\mathbb{G}_{VE}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:DAGtikz}A) indicates that parasite infection should be independent of vaccination if adjusted for diet, reproductive status, sex, and habitat ($P \upmodels V \mid D, R, S, H$); therefore, assuming linearity, we fitted a binomial generalised linear mixed model $P \sim \alpha + \beta_V V + \beta_D D + \beta_R R + \beta_S S + \beta_H H + (1 | ID)$, and confirmed that the posterior distribution of $\beta_V$ was not significantly different from zero. This process was repeated for all implied independencies derived from the causal graph $\mathbb{G}_{VE}$ (see \nameref{sec:structure} methods), and $\mathbb{G}_{VE}$ was updated until all causal paths were both supported by the observed data and consistent with biological expectations. The final DAG $\mathbb{G}_{VE}$ and structural equations $\mathbb{C}_{VE}$ are shown in figure \ref{fig:DAGtikz}.

Using the resulting, validated, DAG $\mathbb{G}_{VE}$, we fit the set of structural equations $\mathbb{C}_{VE}$ (Model \ref{eq:SCM}) as Bayesian hierarchical linear models to estimate the total and direct causal effects indicated by the edges in $\mathbb{G}_{VE}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:DAGtikzParam}).
The posterior distributions of causal parameters (regression coefficients) indicated that vaccination had an average causal effect of 1.50 ± 0.13 ($log10 + 1$)-transformed OD units relative to unvaccinated animal and that vaccine responsiveness was strongly affected by both wild habitat and dietary supplementation. However, while body fat $F$, body mass $M$, and reproductive status $R$ were strongly impacted by Diet and Habitat, they did not mediate those effects onto vaccine responsiveness.

\subsection{Habitat had wide-ranging effects on wood mice and their response to vaccination}

Living in the wild had a negative total effect on vaccine-specific IgG1 of -0.46 ± 0.24 $logOD$, corresponding to a \sim 40\% drop in vaccine responsiveness, and a direct negative impact of -0.66 ± 0.15 $logOD$ once all other mediating factors were adjusted for (Fig.~\ref{fig:DAGtikzParam}A).

Wood mice in the wild but not in the lab could freely reproduce and were naturally exposed to \textit{H. polygyrus}, reflected in direct causal effects of 0.66 ± 0.05 and 0.52 ± 0.08 of $H$ on $R$ and $P$, respectively.

\subsection{Diet supplementation reduced vaccine responsiveness}

The total causal effect of diet supplementation on vaccine responsiveness was estimated to be -0.25 ± 0.09 $logOD$, adjusted for habitat. This corresponds to an approximate 22\% reduction in vaccine responsiveness due to diet alone. However, the direct effect of diet supplementation on vaccine responsiveness was estimated to be -0.23 ± 0.09 $logOD$, when adjusting for body mass, fat stores, habitat, sex, reproductive status, parasite burden, and vaccine formulation. This indicates that the effect of diet supplementation on vaccine responsiveness was mostly direct (or mediated by unobserved variables along the path $D \rightarrow E$, e.g. immune pathways affecting DTV-specific IgG1 production or the microbiome), but was not mediated by its effects on body mass, fat stores, reproductive status, or parasite burden.
Dietary supplementation increased body mass on average by 2.53 ± 0.3 g, though did not affect body fat scores.

\subsection{Sex had both direct and indirect effects on vaccine responsiveness}

The total causal effect of sex on DTV-specific antibody production $E$ indicated greater vaccine responsiveness in females (0.26 ± 0.15 $logOD$). However, only a little more than half of the effect of sex (0.15 ± 0.10 $logOD$) was direct, with body mass (0.08 ± 0.01 $logOD$) and body fat (0.03 ± 0.005 $logOD$) mediating the rest (Fig.~\ref{fig:DAGtikzParam}) as males were both larger and had higher body fat.

\subsection{Parasite infection reduced vaccine responsiveness}

When considering the entire population, including uninfected mice, \textit{H. polygyrus} burden had no clear effect on DT vaccine responsiveness (-0.13 ± 0.11 $logOD$). However, because parasite burdens (measured as worm burdens) are overdispersed and zero-inflated, we also estimated the causal effects of infection burden on DT vaccine responsiveness only among the infected. Here we found that parasite burdens among the infected mice had a strong negative effect on vaccine responsiveness, with a slope of -0.58 ± 0.35 $logOD$, after adjusting for diet, sex, reproductive status, and vaccination. This indicates that higher parasite burdens are associated with a significant decrease in vaccine responsiveness.

\subsection{Prediction of the effect of population-wide anthelmintic treatment on vaccine responsiveness}

Parametrised SCMs allow predicting how interventions on a set of variables might affect the values of downstream, causally posterior, variables for any individual on the population. Here, we wanted to (i) quantify how effective the vaccine might have been, had the whole population been treated against worms (i.e. all worm burdens held at 0 in the modified SCM), and (ii) identify which individuals would have seen the greatest effect of anthelmintic treatment on their individual response to the vaccine. To answer these counterfactual questions, we used our SCM as parametrised with the observed values reported above, and substituted non-zero parasite counts with zeros to generate synthetic counterfactual outcomes, all other variables remaining intact.

We found that, had all mice been treated including those that had not received treatment in our experiment, would have resulted in an overall 15\% increase in vaccine responsiveness, had all mice been treated with anthelmintic drugs (Fig. \ref{fig:post_anthelminthic}A).

The predicted difference in vaccine responsiveness varied between individuals, though not proportionally with observed parasite burdens (Fig. \ref{fig:post_anthelminthic}B). Instead, the strongest predictor of the effect of anthelmintic treatment on vaccine responsiveness was individual reproductive status, followed by diet (Fig. \ref{supfig:Ediff}). This suggests that reproductive individuals may benefit most from anthelmintic treatment for improving vaccine responsiveness.

\section{Discussion}

Using a novel wild-to-lab rodent system in combination with a robust structural casual modelling approach, we provide a rare test of how key intrinsic and environmental factors can impact vaccine responsiveness to a standard Diphtheria toxoid vaccine.  We found that there was substantial variation in vaccine efficacy between individuals in vaccine responsiveness measured by vaccine-specific IgG1 antibodies. In addition, mice living in natural populations in the wild, had on average 46.9 \pm{} 0.1 lower antibodies than individuals living in the sterile, laboratory setting. Counter to our predictions, we also demonstrated that high-quality dietary supplementation actually resulted in lower vaccine responsiveness in mice living in both habitats.
However, we did find that among individuals currently infected with gastrointestinal parasites, there was a negative effect of parasite burden on vaccine responsiveness. Our results emphasise the importance of accounting for the interplay between environmental and individual factors when analysing and predicting responses to immunisation, consistent with reports across multiple habitats and species, including humans~\cite{Zimmermann2019, VanLoveren2001, Kampmann2015, Edwards2018}.

Indeed, there was marked hyporesponsiveness in wild wood mouse populations relative to their laboratory conspecifics immunised with the same Diphtheria Toxoid vaccine formulations. The detrimental effects of the natural habitat on vaccine responsiveness were only partially rescued by a booster dose of the vaccine. This reduction is consistent with widespread reports of vaccine failure during clinical trials, notably in phase III where vaccines are tested for efficacy in naturally exposed individuals~\cite{Pronker2013}, or in already-established vaccines when used in rural populations~\cite{vanDorst2024, vanRiet2007a}.
In our study, part of the loss of vaccine responsiveness in the wild habitat was mediated by the gastrointestinal nematode \textit{H. polygyrus}. This negative effect of high worm burdens on vaccine responsiveness is consistent with reports in humans and other animals that have shown that parasitic helminths can negatively impact vaccination~\cite{Elias2008, deRuiter2020, Tweyongyere2019, Cooper2001b, Urban2007}. These reports are not unanimous, however, with many reporting no~\cite{Bruckner2015} or even a positive association between vaccine responsiveness and parasitic infection~\cite{Wait2020, Natukunda2022}. While parasite infection can have varied effects on vaccine responsiveness, our study suggests that conflicting inferences might be due to how the effects of parasite infection are estimated. Specifically, we only detected a negative effect of parasite burden among the infected animals, and no effect when uninfected individuals were included in the analysis. Thus, we might predict that vaccination campaigns that include anthelmintic treatment as part of their regime, could lead to detectable population-wide improvements in vaccine efficacy for populations where nematode infection prevalence and burdens are high. While we were unable to investigate the mechanisms by which \textit{H. polygyrus} affected vaccine responsiveness, two hypotheses have been proposed. The first suggests counter-regulation between different arms of the immune system, e.g., Th2 responses might suppress pathways that drive vaccine-specific antibody production\cite{Cao2016,Mosmann1996}, while the second hypothesis is focused on general immune suppression by the parasites. Animal studies have highlighted the immunoregulatory effects of gastrointestinal parasites, in particular of \textit{H. bakeri}~\cite{Maizels2016, Maizels2012a}. Here, the immune system of wood mice naturally infected by \textit{H. polygyrus}, which is distinct from the laboratory model~\cite{Stevens2023a}, might similarly be actively suppressed by the parasite. Alternatively, animals inherently more susceptible to helminth infection might have a generally lower propensity to generate strong antibody responses, which we detect as poor vaccine responsiveness. Resolving these alternatives would require testing the prediction of both vaccine responsiveness and nematode burden from immune profiling before both infection and vaccination (see for example \cite{Tsang2014, Kotliarov2020}).
While our study focussed on nematodes, other infections are likely to have impacted vaccine responsiveness in the wild, including viruses, bacteria, and other helminths. In particular, reports that variability in responses to SARS-CoV2 in humans could be explained by cytomegalovirus infections~\cite{Aquino2023}, and given the 10–70\% prevalence of wood mouse herpesvirus infections in our study populations~\cite{Knowles2012}, it is likely that this virus (or other parasites/pathogens) could have also impacted vaccine responsiveness in our study.

Dietary supplementation with a high-quality mouse chow had strong but surprising impacts on the immune response. First, we found that access to dietary supplementation in the wild, or a higher quality mouse chow in the laboratory)  caused a loss of vaccine responsiveness. This was surprising, as based on our earlier work showing increased immunity to parasite infection~\cite{Sweeny2021}, we had predicted that diet supplementation would similarly increase vaccine responsiveness. Second, we found that diet supplementation had no detectable effect on reducing parasite burdens. This effect contrasts with our previous study, where parasite burdens were reduced by approximately half~\cite{Sweeny2021} when provided access to this high-quality diet. This difference may be due to temporal variation in the availability of natural food resources during this experiment, such that mice that were not given supplementation had more abundant natural food availability than in earlier studies, eliminating the positive impact of high-quality nutrition on antihelminth immunity.
The combined detrimental effect on vaccine responsiveness and the positive or null effect of dietary supplementation on antihelminth immunity indicate that nutrition differently affects immune pathways underlying vaccine responses and anthelmintic immunity. We therefore hypothesise that the immune system adopts different strategies depending on the availability and composition of nutritional resources. Indeed, this would comport with expectations under life history theory and host-helminth co-evolution~\cite{Tate2015,Babayan2010}, where under resource constraints one should expect the suppression of non-existential phenotypes, i.e., of ``luxury phenotypes''~\cite{Geist1989}. Indeed, anthelmintic immunity, unlike antiviral or antibacterial immunity, may be one such luxury phenotype. Under this hypothesis, investment in complete immunity to helminths (reliant on type 2 immune pathways) is expected when nutritional resources are abundant; conversely, under resource limitation, only inflammatory and type 1 immunity would be maintained. Vaccines that benefit from inflammatory and type 1 immunity would generate stronger vaccine-specific immune responses. This is consistent with, and might provide a coherent account of, our and other's previous studies reporting a pro-inflammatory effect of protein scarcity~\cite{Ing2000, Coltherd2009b}. The elucidation of the mechanisms mediating these effects, specifically of the differential impact of nutrition on different immune pathways and functions, warrants further investigation.

Females overall had higher vaccine responsiveness than males, partly as a direct effect of sex and partly mediated by body mass and fat content. Sexual dimorphism in disease susceptibility and immunity has been  widely reported across multiple host and pathogen species, with females typically more resistant to infection including by helminths~\cite{Klein2004}, generating stronger responses to vaccination, but more likely to develop autoimmunity~\cite{Flanagan2017,Klein2012,Hepworth2010,Scully2020,Wanelik2023}. Large-scale studies have identified sexual dimorphism in specific immune pathways, with females tending to present higher activation of innate pathways~\cite{Gal-Oz2019}, though this might be especially true in younger individuals~\cite{Marquez2020}. In our study, mice tended to be in the early adult stage, possibly skewing younger in the wild cohort where lifespans are likely to be much shorter, though this effect was vastly overshadowed by other negative effects on vaccine responsiveness. After adjusting for habitat, while females had lower body mass and fat stores, these differences had no effect on DTV-specific antibody responses. In fact, we only detected a direct effect of sex, and no indirect effects.

Finally, we utilised the fully parameterised SCM to estimate how vaccine responsiveness would have differed, had all mice been treated against helminths. We found that population-wide anthelmintic treatment would have increased vaccine responsiveness by 15\% on average. This suggests that anthelmintic treatment could be a useful intervention to improve vaccine responsiveness in populations where helminth infections are prevalent. This is consistent with reports that anthelmintic treatment can improve vaccine responsiveness in humans~\cite{Cooper2001b, Elias2008, Tweyongyere2019, Urban2007}, and suggests that anthelmintic treatment could be a useful intervention to improve vaccine responsiveness in populations where helminth infections are prevalent. More specifically, the greatest beneficiaries of anthelmintic treatment are predicted to be the reproductive individuals. This effect appeared to affect both sexes equally, which might be due to \textit{A. sylvaticus} being a species whose males exhibit unusually high reproductive investment~\cite{Corp1997,Harrison2010}, perhaps on par with female investment in pregnancy and lactation. Generalising beyond this system, we would thus hypothesise that prioritisation of reproductive individuals for anthelmintic treatment might provide the greatest impact in improving vaccine responsiveness. However, in cases where pregnant and breastfeeding women are excluded from anthelmintic drug treatments~\cite{Santiso1997,Elliott2011}, targeted vaccine boosters might help rescue loss of efficacy (see `DD' regimen in Fig.~\ref{fig:igg1}A).

Importantly, our study was limited to variables that could be measured in a wild, non-model, species. Further studies are needed to disentangle how different arms of the immune system mediate the effects of vaccination on antibody production, and how the environmental factors may modulate those different pathways. We also focussed on a single antibody isotype, IgG1. While this is appropriate for assessing alum-adjuvanted vaccines in a neutralising antibody scenario, further studies should investigate protective immunity to specific pathogens after vaccination. Indeed, while our conclusions are consistent with reports that investigate protective immunity~\cite{vanDorst2024}, it is likely that inferences would differ depending on the pathogens targeted and the vaccination formulations employed. Aside from the immune system, it might be useful to also explore how the gut microbiota might mediate the dietary effects, and how different nutrients, in quality and abundance whether supplied or available in the environment, might drive these interactions that impact vaccine responsiveness.

Lastly, taken together, this study provides a useful methodological approach to estimate causal relationships between environmental and individual factors and vaccine responsiveness. This approach could be used to predict vaccine responsiveness in target populations, and to identify which populations or subsets are likely to respond poorly to a specific vaccine, and/or benefit from targeted interventions such as diet changes, antiparasitic treatments, or different vaccine adjuvants/regimes. This could be particularly useful for vaccines that are known to be sensitive to environmental factors, such as anthelmintic vaccines, and for which the causes of vaccine hyporesponsiveness remain difficult to identify, quantify, and therefore, address. Our study also indicates that laboratory studies may not always be generalisable to wild populations, and are likely to systematically overestimate vaccine efficacy. This suggests that the development of vaccines should be more closely aligned with the conditions in which they are intended to be used, and that the effects of environmental factors on vaccine responsiveness should be more thoroughly investigated.

\section{Materials, Methods, and Models} \label{sec:methods}

All animal work was conducted in compliance with the UK Home Office Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act, 1986. All laboratory and field experiments were approved by the University of Edinburgh Ethical Review Committee and carried out under the UK Home Office Project Licence 70/8543. The dosage of diphtheria vaccine was tested in laboratory-bred wood mice before the experiments for safety. Animal sacrifice was performed using appropriate Schedule 1 Methods. Fieldwork was carried out with permission of the Forestry Commission Scotland under the permit SUR09.

\subsection{Vacccine formulation and administration}

The vaccine used in this study was a commercially available diphtheria inactivated toxoid (DT, Alpha Diagnostic International). The vaccine, DTV henceforth, was prepared in our laboratory one day before administration, using 2 Lf of vaccine-grade DT protein adsorbed to the adjuvant (9\% alum). The vaccine was administered subcutaneously in a volume of 100μl. The control group was injected with 100μl of adjuvant (9\% alum) suspended in 1X PBS.

\subsection{Laboratory wood mouse experiments; diet and vaccination regime}
The laboratory experiments were carried out on a colony of wild-derived but now captive outbred wood mouse colony bred and maintained at the University of Edinburgh~\cite{Sweeny2021}. The experiment was conducted in two replicate blocks, with 36 animals in each block (18 males, 18 females). At 12 days before the start of the experiment (d-12), all mice were shifted to new diet regimes and given time to acclimatise; half of the animals in each block (9 males and 9 females) were given TransBreed\textsuperscript{TM}, a high quality whole-diet mouse food. We have previously shown that wood mice supplemented with TransBreed\textsuperscript{TM} are more resistant to the gastrointestinal nematode \textit{Heligmosomoides polygyrus}, cleared worms more effectively after anthelmintic treatment, and produced stronger general (total IgA) and parasite-specific (IgG) immune responses in both wild and laboratory conditions~\cite{Sweeny2021}. The other half of the mice in this experiment were fed RM1 (Rat and Mouse Maintenance 1). Both TransBreed\textsuperscript{TM} and RM1 are standard laboratory diets, with TransBreed\textsuperscript{TM} containing higher amounts of starch, protein, fat, and micronutrients compared to RM1. Within each diet regime, both male and female mice were randomly assigned to one of three experimental groups (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}). On d0, animals belonging to Groups `DD' \& `DA' (n = 24) were subcutaneously injected with 100μl of DTV, and Group `AD' (n = 12) were injected with the adjuvant control. Small blood samples were taken via tail snip on d14 and d17 to measure the primary antibody response. Twenty-one days (d21) after the first injections, the animals of Group `DD' (n = 12) were injected again with 100μl of DTV (`DD': diphtheria vaccine followed by diphtheria booster), while those assigned to Group `DA' (n= 12) were injected with the control dose as described above (`DA': DTV followed by adjuvant only). Animals assigned to Group `AD' (n = 12) that had previously been injected only with the control adjuvant only dose now received an injection of 100μl DTV (`AD': adjuvant followed by DTV). Another blood sample (20-50μl) was taken a day later (d22) by cheek venipuncture. All animals were sacrificed on d35 (i.e., 14 days after the second injection) and a blood sample was taken for measuring the antibody response. The same procedures and timeline were carried out for the second experimental block of animals (n = 36). Mice were co-housed throughout the experiment, in same-sex groups of three, with equal representation of the three experimental groups in each cage to prevent confounding cage effects.

\subsection{Wild wood mice experiment}

We conducted a 10-week field experiment in a natural population of wood mice with a design similar to that of the laboratory experiment. The experiment was conducted in a woodland in Falkirk, Scotland, UK (Callendar Wood, 55.990470, -3.766636), where we established four trapping grids (60m \times{} 40m per grid) with 10m spacing between each of the 35 trapping stations per grid. At each station, we set a pair of Sherman live traps (H.B. Sherman 2 \times{} 2.5 \times{} 6.5-inch folding trap, Tallahassee, FL, USA). We randomly selected two of the four grids for dietary supplementation, initiating the treatment 12 days before live-trapping began. Specifically, 6kg of TransBreed\textsuperscript{TM} (a high-quality, whole-diet mouse food) pellets were evenly scattered across each treated grid, with supplementation continuing twice weekly throughout the experiment. The remaining two grids served as controls and received no additional food. All wood mice had access to their standard diet, so the supplemental pellets on the treated grids provided an extra source of high-quality nutrition.

From July-September 2018, wood mice were trapped 2–3 nights per week using live traps baited with grains, carrots, and bedding. Additionally, traps on the supplemented grids were also baited with 1-2 TransBreed\textsuperscript{TM} pellets. For identification, all newly captured mice weighing >13g were subcutaneously tagged with a unique 9-digit microchip passive induced transponder (PIT tag; FriendChip AVID2028, Norco, CA, USA). Upon first capture, mice were randomly assigned to one of the three vaccine treatment groups `DD', `DA', and `AD', described above and in Fig.~\ref{fig:design}; mice assigned to these groups received the same primary and booster doses of DTV or alum control as described above. Throughout the experiment, small volume blood samples were taken weekly via tail snip from each tagged animal to measure their antibody response to the diphtheria immunisation, on days as close to possible as their laboratory counterparts. Animals recaptured 14 or more days after their second injection were sacrificed and a terminal bleed was collected to measure their antibody response and adult \textit{Heligmosomoides polygyrus} worm burdens. \textit{H. polygyrus} is a natural gastrointestinal nematode found at high prevalence (20-100 \%) in wild populations of \textit{A. sylvaticus}~\cite{Sweeny2021,Gregory1990,Keymer1987,Knowles2013}, as well as being a well-studied model system of human gastrointestinal nematodes where it has been found to be highly immunomodulatory~\cite{Maizels2012a,Reynolds2012a}.

Additionally, at all captures, we took the following demographic metrics for each individual: sex, body condition, reproductive condition, weight (g), and length (mm). Sex and reproductive condition of each mouse was assigned by examining the genitals, with males having larger urogenital gap compared to females. Males were classified as reproductively active if their testes had visibly descended to scrotal sacs, and females were classified as reproductively active if they had a perforated vagina or were visibly pregnant or lactating. Body condition was measured by assigning dorsal and pelvic fat scores on a qualitative scale from 1-5, where 1 represented a mouse with very low condition/fat reserves, while a 5 represented a mouse with ample fat reserves. This was achieved by palpating the back and pubic bones~\cite{Ullman-Cullere1999}. Both scores were added to provide a single metric of body condition for analysis.

\subsection{Vaccine responsiveness: DT-specific IgG1}

Vaccine responsiveness was measured as the concentration of anti-diphtheria IgG1 antibodies. This is the gold standard method used in vaccinology to measure the antibody-specific response to immunisation and is used specifically for the diphtheria toxoid vaccine used here~\cite{Faruq2010}. Blood samples from both the laboratory and the wild mice were centrifuged @24,000 rpm for 10 min within 4–6 hours of collection. The sera were separated from the pellets and stored at -80 °C until further analysis. 96-well plates (NuncTM MicroWellTM) were coated overnight at 4 °C with diphtheria toxoid (2μg/mL) diluted in carbonate buffer (50 μl per well). After washing the plates with TBS (10 X) and Tween80 thrice, 100 μl of TBS(1X)-4\% BSA was added per well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 hours to block non-specific binding sites. Plates were then tapped dry and 50 μl of serum samples serially diluted (starting dilution 1:100) in TBS (1X)-4\% BSA buffer were added per well and left at 4 °C for binding overnight. The next day, 50 μl HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG1 detection antibody (Southern BioTech) per well were added after washing the plates 4 times with TBS (10X) and Tween80 and tapping them dry. After incubation at 37 °C for 1 hour, the plates were washed again, four times with TBS (10X) and Tween80 and then twice with dH20. Then, 50 μl of TMB substrate solution were added per well and the enzymatic reaction was left to develop in the dark for 7 min. The reaction was stopped after 7 min using 50 μl sulphuric acid (0.18 M) per well. Absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using an ELISA plate reader (Multiscan, Ascent Labsystems) immediately thereafter. For each sample, blank-centred optical densities (OD) of three consecutive wells (of dilutions 1:3200, 1:6400, 1:12800) were averaged to obtain diphtheria-specific IgG antibody measurements for each individual.

\subsection{Data processing and analyses}

ELISA optical densities of diphtheria (DT)-specific antibodies ($E$) were $1 + log_{10}$-trans\-formed and, body weight ($M$) and fat scores ($F$) were Z score-transformed to help the selection of sensible priors and to improve computational stability. All other variables were treated as binary. All data processing was performed in Julia v1.11~\cite{Bezanson2017} using packages CSV.jl~\cite{CSV2022} and DataFrames.jl~\cite{DataFrames2022}. All code is available at \url{https://github.com/SimonAB/Apodemus_vaccines}\todo{Make the repo public; in the meanwhile, you can find the code in the \texttt{src} directory.}

\subsubsection{Generalised linear mixed models of the effects of experimental interventions of habitat, diet, and vaccine formulation on vaccine responsiveness}\label{sec:GLMM}

Generalised linear mixed models (GLMM) were used to estimate the effects of habitat, diet, and vaccine formulation on vaccine-specific antibody concentrations measured as optical density read-outs. Mouse ID was modelled as a random effect to account for multiple testing. The interactions between vaccine regime and habitat, and between diet and habitat, were included in the full model to test whether wild mice responded differently to the vaccine formulations or to diet supplementation than laboratory mice. Likelihood ratio tests (LRT) were used to evaluate the contribution of the interaction terms to the model. The package MixedModels.jl~\cite{douglas_bates_2022} was used for all GLMMs and LRTs.

We then used Bayesian hierarchical models to minimise the effects of imbalance on our analyses and to quantify the uncertainty of those estimates. Weakly informative Gaussian priors were chosen for the intercept and slope (see \nameref{supdata:MultilevelModels}). Population-level residuals were assumed to follow an exponential distribution. Parameters for group level intercepts and coefficients were given Gaussian priors and their variances were given positive-truncated Cauchy priors. Posterior estimates were sampled using the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo algorithm No U-Turn Sampler \cite{Hoffman2014}, typically across 4 threads simultaneously with $> 3,000$ draws. Turing.jl~\cite{ge2018t} was used for Bayesian modelling; Makie.jl v0.17~\cite{Danisch2021} was used for plotting.

\subsubsection{Structural Causal Models}

The structural causal modelling (SCM) framework~\cite{Pearl2009b,Peters2017a} proposes explicit qualitative solutions to biases inherent in datasets and can guide the statistical estimation of the causal mechanisms by which data are generated in complex systems~\cite{Bareinboim2016,Pearl2012b}. Further, Bayesian statistical methods for estimating the causal effects allow unbalanced small data and beetter imputation of missing values~\cite{McElreath2020}, and provide explicit estimation and propagation of uncertainty given assumptions made by the model~\cite{Gelman2013}. Specifically, here we have made three strong assumptions: that vaccine, diet, and habitat treatments were randomly allocated; that there were no unmeasured (statistically relevant) confounders; and that all relationships between variables were linear.

\subsubsection{Model construction}\label{sec:construction}

To understand more specifically the relative contributions of diet and habitat to vaccine responsiveness, and how these effects might be mediated by parasite infection, reproductive status, and body condition, we built a causal model of vaccine efficacy in our wood mouse system, first by constructing a graphical model of our hypotheses, then using our data to validate and refine its structure (see \nameref{sec:structure} and casual flow diagram, Fig.~\ref{supfig:SCM_flow}).

Vaccination $V$ was allocated at random, and therefore has no parent node. It was hypothesised to affect parasite infection $P$, reproductive status $R$, body mass $M$ and fat stores $F$ through non-specific effects in addition to its main intended effect on vaccine responsiveness $E$.
Habitat $H$ was considered random and therefore took no parent node, but was hypothesised to affect all other variables except $V$ and sex $S$.
Likewise, diet supplementation $D$ was allocated at random, and was hypothesised to affect reproductive status $R$, body mass $M$, body fat $F$ and parasite burdens $P$ as well as $E$, with fat scores $F$ hypothesised to be a cause of variation in body mass $M$.
Sex $S$ being genetically determined and therefore subject to Mendelian randomisation~\cite{Lawlor2008}, and all treatments being randomised with respect to sex, $S$ had no parent node, and was considered causally prior to reproductive status $R$, body mass $M$, body fat $F$ and parasite burdens $P$.
Reproductive status $R$ was hypothesised to affect body mass $M$ and parasite burden $P$.
Vaccine responsiveness $E$ was hypothesised to be affected by all other variables.
Each node also had its independent error term to capture all unobserved sources of variation, assumed to be independent and identically distributed (Figure~\ref{fig:DAGtikz}B).

Model construction was performed in Julia v1.11~\cite{Bezanson2017} using package Dagitty.jl and Turing.jl~\cite{ge2018t}, and $\mathbb{G}_{VE}$ corresponding to $\mathbb{C}_{VE}$ was drawn in Tikz~\cite{Tan12}.

\subsubsection{Model validation}\label{sec:structure}

To validate $\mathbb{C}_{VE}$ (Model~\ref{eq:SCM}), we tested the independencies it implies~\cite{Pearl2009b,Cinelli2021}. Specifically, we used the observed values for each of the nodes of the causal graph (Figure~\ref{fig:DAGtikz}) to test the truth of the following 13 implied independences: ($D \upmodels S$), ($D \upmodels T \mid H$), ($D \upmodels V$), ($F \upmodels V \mid T, H$), ($H \upmodels S$), ($H \upmodels V$), ($M \upmodels V \mid T, H$), ($P \upmodels T \mid D, R, S, H$), ($P \upmodels V \mid T, H$), ($P \upmodels V \mid D, R, S, H$), ($R \upmodels V \mid T, H$), ($S \upmodels T$), and ($S \upmodels V$). For each of these tests, we constructed a generalised linear mixed model with the appropriate link function, with mouse ID and immunisation protocol as random effects, and considered the statement true if $P_X \greater 0.1$, where $X \subseteq \mathbb{C}_{VE}$ (\nameref{supdata:SCMconstruction}). We used MixedModels.jl ~\cite{douglas_bates_2022} to test the conditional independencies implied by $\mathbb{C}_{VE}$.
The unmeasured noise variables $\mathcal{U}_j$ (Model~\ref{eq:SCM}), were assumed to be independent and identically distributed.

\subsubsection{Model parameterisation, statistical estimation, ad simulation}\label{sec:parameterisation}

Bayesian models were used for statistical estimation of causal effects, assuming linearity and independence of noise variables as above. All Bayesian modelling was performed in Julia v1.11~\cite{Bezanson2017} using Turing.jl~\cite{ge2018t}.

\paragraph{Statistical identification.} We encoded $\mathbb{C}_{VE}$ as a Bayesian network to represent each variable as a posterior distribution to be updated by the observed values. Models were constructed as described in \nameref{sec:GLMM}, code provided in \nameref{supdata:SCMspecification}. The posterior distribution of each parameter was estimated using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with the No U-Turn Sampler~\cite{Hoffman2014}, sampled 3000 times over 4 chains.

\paragraph{Missing data.} Twenty-two mice had no fat scores, resulting in 67 missing values in the full repeated sampling dataset. To avoid having to remove those mice from the analysis, we used Bayesian imputation to estimate plausible probability distributions of each of the missing values given the values of parent nodes in $\mathbb{C}_{VE}$ (Model~\ref{eq:SCM}) for each of the 22 mice. For example, missing fat scores were imputed as $ \dot F \sim \alpha + \beta_T \cdot T + \beta_H \cdot H + \beta_D \cdot D + \beta_P \cdot P + \beta_S \cdot S$, where $\dot F$ is the vector of fat scores containing missing values, $\alpha$ is the intercept and each of the $\beta$ parameters are the partial regression coefficients of the linear model (Figure~\ref{fig:DAGtikz}).

\paragraph{Counterfactual estimation of anthelmintic treatment.} Do quantify vaccine responsiveness in mice, had they been fully de-wormed at the time of immunisation, we fixed all values of parasite counts $P$ at 0, resulting in the blocking of all paths into $P$, thereby simulating the blocking of causal effects mediated by or originating from \textit{H. polygyrus} infection. Comparing parameter values of unmodified (true) and modified (counterfactual) scenarios allowed (a) estimation of the population-wide change in vaccine responsiveness, had all mice been administered the anthelmintic, and (b) identification of the covariates associated with the greatest change in vaccine responsiveness at the individual level, using the individual differences between observed pre- and predicted post-intervention values of $E$ as the response variable of a generalised linear mixed effects model with the explanatory variables indicated by $\mathbb{G}_{VE}$.
